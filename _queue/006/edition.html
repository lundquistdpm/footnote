Building in public · Essay

I don't write the first draft. I own the last one.

Every edition of The Footnote so far began as a draft I did not write. An AI agent searched the journals and the trade press, read the sources, and wrote in a voice built from my own rules. Then I did the part that carries my name. I read every sentence, opened every link, and fixed what was wrong.

Most people who write with AI keep it quiet. I think quiet is the wrong instinct for physicians. We already sign notes that someone else drafted, first human scribes and now ambient ones. The signature has always meant the same thing: I checked this, and it is mine. A newsletter works the same way.

So this edition shows the whole machine. You will see the steps, the constraints I put on the agent, the places it still gets things wrong, and why I think this way of working is the clearest preview we have of the physician this profession will need next. Nothing here requires a computer science degree. It requires the same habits we learned in residency, applied to a new kind of assistant.

The pipeline, step by step

Six steps, in the order they happen. The tag on each one shows who does the work.

Agent Claude, through the Anthropic API Code scripts and scheduled jobs Me the part I will not hand off
  1. Pick the thesis

    I choose what the edition argues and who it is for. The resident edition, the profession edition, this one. The agent never chooses the story. It serves one.

    Me
  2. Research six beats

    The agent searches the web for each beat: clinical, surgical research, policy, practice business, products, and voices. It works from a brief that ranks ACFAS, the Journal of Foot & Ankle Surgery, AOFAS, and Foot & Ankle International ahead of everything else. It is told to skip press releases dressed as news, and to return nothing when nothing is worth running.

    Agent
  3. Draft in my voice

    Every summary is written from scratch, never copied from the source. The draft follows a written style guide, shown below, that the agent cannot negotiate with.

    Agent
  4. Check the citations

    Journal references run through PubMed and Crossref to confirm the paper exists and says what we claim. This pipeline started on my bunion patient education site, where a fabricated reference would reach a patient.

    Code
  5. Read, verify, correct

    I open every link and read every line against its source. I have corrected follower counts, rankings, and a colleague's title. Some weeks the fix is a number. Some weeks it is a whole opening paragraph that said the right thing in the wrong tone.

    Me
  6. Build and publish

    The edition renders into a fixed template. A script blocks any sponsor from running in an edition that covers its own product. The finished page waits in a queue, publishes itself Monday at 6 AM Central, and the email follows at 7 so the link is already live.

    Code

The rules the agent writes under

An AI draft sounds generic when nobody gave it a spine. These are the constraints written into every run. Several of them exist because I caught the agent breaking them once.

Voice
  • Start with an admission or an observation, then the insight
  • One thesis per piece
  • No questions aimed at the reader
  • No sales pitch in the body copy
  • No em-dashes and no emoji
  • Close on who the reader is, never on a question
Evidence and money
  • Never invent a URL, a publication, or a statistic
  • Every figure links to where it came from
  • If a page blocks automated reading, flag the detail for me to confirm
  • Disclose my roles whenever a story touches them
  • One sponsor per edition, labeled, and it never sees a story before publication

Why an AI-built newsletter can be better

The trade newsletters most of us open were built for a different era. Editorial hours were the scarce resource, so the format became a stack of links: a paragraph borrowed from the source, a Read More button, and a display ad between each item. One podiatry newsletter I read this week ran twelve display ads around roughly a dozen items. The people who make those newsletters work hard. The format limits them.

An agent changes what is scarce. Research that took a staff a day now takes minutes, so a single physician's time can go where it matters: judgment, verification, and a point of view.

The honest label

People have started calling this an AI startup. I checked that claim the way I would check any other. Investors now use a simple test to separate AI-native companies from AI-enabled ones: remove the AI and see what happens. If the work slows down, the company is AI-enabled. If the work stops, it is AI-native.

Passes

AI-native

Turn off the agent and the weekly edition stops. A surgeon with a clinic cannot research six beats every week by hand. The AI is the foundation of this newsletter, not a feature added to it.

Not yet

Startup

The Footnote has no investors and no staff. It is not a venture-backed company, and I will not call it one. What it shares with AI-native startups is how it is built.

Y Combinator partner Tom Blomfield describes an AI-native company as a loop, and this newsletter runs on the same one.

The physician this points to

Physicians have already adopted AI. The AMA found that 81% of physicians used it in practice in 2026, up from 38% in 2023. The same survey found that 85% want a say in how their organizations adopt it, and 88% worry about losing skills to it. Medical education is moving too. On September 8, ACGME proposed residency requirements that name artificial intelligence twice, under professionalism and under systems-based practice. Podiatric residencies answer to CPME rather than ACGME, but the direction is plain.

Using AI is no longer what sets a physician apart. What sets a physician apart is running it well: knowing what to hand off, checking what comes back, saying so out loud, and taking responsibility for the result. I teach that as four gates, and this newsletter passes through all four every week.

DelegateHand the volume to the tool.Here: the agent does the research and the first draft.
VerifyCheck the output before it reaches anyone.Here: code checks the citations, then I check everything.
DiscloseSay when AI helped, in plain words.Here: this edition, and a disclosure line on every one.
OwnYour name means you answer for it.Here: my byline, and my corrections in public.

A newsletter is a low-stakes place to practice. Nobody's foot depends on a headline. That is exactly why it makes a good proving ground. The habits carry straight to prior authorization letters, patient education, ambient notes, and whatever arrives next in the clinic. The podiatrists who build those habits now will run small teams of agents the way we already run a surgical team: clear roles, standard steps, and one person accountable at the end.

The podiatrists who shape the next decade will be the ones who let the machine draft and never let it sign.

Still imperfect

What the agent gets wrong

It sometimes second-guesses a fact I have already confirmed. Several society and trade sites block automated reading, so some details arrive secondhand and need my check. Job listings expire between the draft and the send. It also cannot publish to LinkedIn, because LinkedIn offers no way to do that.

The line I hold

What I will not automate

The thesis of each edition. The final read. Any decision about a patient. The agent makes me faster at the work I would have done anyway. It does not do the part that requires a license.
